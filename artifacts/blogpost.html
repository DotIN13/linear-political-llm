Visual Language Models & Political Ideology

What Images “Think” About Politics

We probe a visual language model with 25,000 stock photos and hundreds of Congressional portraits. The result: a coherent liberal–conservative axis encoded in pixel-level features—and it aligns surprisingly well with probes built from text alone.

Tianyi Zhang · 15 min read · Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct  |  DW-NOMINATE 

Does a visual language model see politics in a photograph of jellyfish? Or in a sand dune with an ATV kicking up rooster tails of dust? The question sounds absurd— and yet, here we are.

Over the past year we have been studying the linear political structure of large language models. Our earlier work showed that a simple ridge-regression probe trained on a model's internal attention-head activations can read off a politician's DW-NOMINATE ideology score from the text they generate. The probe generalizes well across topics, models, and prompts, suggesting the model has learned a coherent latent representation of the liberal–conservative spectrum.

The natural next question: does this structure transfer to vision? When we feed a multimodal model an image instead of text, do the same head activations carry the same ideological signal? And can we learn a probe directly from portraits of politicians—rather than from text about policy—and still recover ideology?

In this post we report two lines of experiments that together paint a consistent picture: yes, visual and textual ideology representations are deeply intertwined inside a vision-language model.

Background

The DW-NOMINATE Probe

DW-NOMINATE is the canonical political-science measure of Congressional voting ideology. Its first dimension (nominate_dim1) places legislators on a liberal (−1) to conservative (+1) axis, estimated from roll-call votes. It is not a survey—it is derived purely from revealed voting behavior on thousands of bills over many Congresses.

Our textual ideology probe works as follows. We prompt a causal language model—here Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct—to respond as a named U.S. politician. During generation we record the per-token hidden state at every attention head (layer × head × d_head). A ridge regression is then fit, across politicians, to predict their DW-NOMINATE score from the first generated token's head activations. This gives us a reusable textual probe that can be tested in visual transfer settings.

Part One

Generalization Check: Text Probe on Congressional Portraits

As our first transfer test, we apply the same text-trained probe to image tokens from official Congressional portraits, with no retraining. If the textual probe captures a stable ideology axis, we hypothesize that it should also separate parties on portraits.

Even without explicitly revealing to the model who is in the portrait, the model assigns noticeably different ideological activations to the two parties. The central image tokens— corresponding to the face and upper body—drive most of the difference, while backgrounds and edge patches are more neutral.

Key finding On a per-portrait basis, Democrat portraits receive a mean image-token score of roughly −0.26, while Republican portraits score around −0.14. Republicans are shifted roughly half a standard deviation toward the conservative pole relative to Democrats, using only pixel information.

Applying the Textual Probe to Images (Portraits + Unsplash)

The Unsplash 25k dataset is a curated sample from Unsplash's open-license photography collection. It covers essentially every subject imaginable: nature, architecture, food, travel, animals, sports, art, and more. These images are not inherently associated with political ideology.

Yet our question is: if we feed each image to Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct with the prompt "Write a possible news article that would best accompany this image, from the best possible media outlet." and extract the attention-head activations at the image tokens, then apply the DW-NOMINATE–trained probe, what scores come out?

Setup

For each of the 24,996 images we obtained a per-patch score vector from the top-k ideology probe heads and averaged across patches to produce a single mean DW score per image. This score is in the same units as the original DW-NOMINATE dimension: negative means more liberal, positive means more conservative.

The distribution is surprisingly well-behaved: tightly centered near zero with a standard deviation of 0.13. The slight liberal lean of the mean may simply reflect the aesthetic character of Unsplash photography, but the full range spans over a full DW-NOMINATE unit.

What Makes an Image “Conservative” or “Liberal”?

The extremes tell the most interesting story.

The model is not “seeing politics”—it seems to be responding to deep visual texture, color palette, and subject matter that statistically co-vary with the trained politicians’ imagery of the world.

The patterns are interpretable to a great extent. High-scoring images share visual themes associated with rural, outdoor, and traditionally “masculine” American recreational culture: off-road vehicles on sandy terrain, big-game trophies, open ranchland, and desert roads. Low-scoring images cluster around saturated color, biological forms (jellyfish, flowers, produce), and urban diversity.

Importantly, these are correlational signals learned from the model's training distribution as reflected in politician response patterns—they are not intentional value judgments encoded by the probe or the researchers. This is likely the effect of the multi-modal pretraining where models learn how to associate images with political symbols both textual and visual.

Topic Clusters & the t-SNE Map

To go beyond individual images we embedded all 24,996 images using pre-computed Unsplash embeddings (encoded with clip-ViT-B-32), clustered them into 10 coarse topics with K-Means, and projected to 2D with t-SNE. Each point's color encodes its mean DW score.

The t-SNE map reveals that ideological signal is not randomly scattered— it clusters with visual topic. The reddest (most conservative) island in the upper portion of the map is almost entirely composed of sand-dune vehicles and arid landscapes. The deepest blue region corresponds to the aquatic/jellyfish and floral photography topics. The broad neutral center is populated by cityscapes, portraits, and general travel photography.

Selected clusters further illustrate the pattern. Cluster 9 (below) pits bioluminescent jellyfish (strongly liberal) against elk and horned lizards (strongly conservative)—two very different wildlife subjects, separated by an enormous DW gap of nearly 1.0 unit.

Part Two

A Probe Built Directly from Portraits

The experiments above used a text-trained probe in transfer mode. Next we ask a stricter question: can we train a probe entirely from portraits—with no policy-text activations involved—and still recover ideology?

Portrait Probe Setup

We assembled official portrait images for members of the 116th House of Representatives (2019–21) and matched each portrait to its owner's DW-NOMINATE score. After filtering for image availability we obtained a dataset of 550 portraits covering both parties.

For each portrait we ran Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct with the prompt "What's his/her position on US politics?" and captured the same attention-head activations at the last token. A ridge regression was then fit on these visual activations with DW-NOMINATE as the target— independently from any text prompt at training time.

This is a genuinely visual probe: the label (ideology) is matched to image activations, not text activations. If the probe is predictive, it means the model encodes ideology-relevant information in how it processes a person's face and backdrop.

The headwise R² map shows this portrait probe underperforms the textual probe, where we were able to reach an R² of over 0.8. Predictive accuracy is distributed across many mid-late heads, suggesting ideology is also represented as a broad linear subspace in portrait mode, yet the image tokens were more noisy than its textual counterpart. The degradation in performance might also be the result of not directly probing on the image tokens residing in the facial area.

Part Three

Do the Two Probes See the Same Thing?

At this point we have two ideology probes for the same model:

  • Textual probe — trained on text activations from politician impersonation prompts.
  • Portrait probe — trained on image-token activations from Congress member portraits.

Both predict DW-NOMINATE ideology, but via completely different inputs. Do they point in the same direction in activation space?

Cosine Similarity of Probe Directions

For each of the 36 × 32 = 1,152 attention heads we computed the cosine similarity between the ridge-regression coefficient vectors of the two probes (weighted by activation standard deviation, to match the magnitude scaling used at inference). A cosine similarity of +1 means the two probes learned exactly the same linear direction in that head's hidden space.

Across all 1,152 heads the mean cosine similarity is approximately +0.4. When we restrict attention to the heads that are strongly predictive (R² ≥ 0.7 for either probe), the alignment is even cleaner—these high-signal heads show predominantly positive cosine similarity, indicating that the same linear direction learned from text also captures ideology from portraits.

The model likely represents ideology along some latent axis, and that axis is accessible from both language and vision.

Co-Firing Analysis: Token-by-Token Agreement

As a third line of evidence, we examined whether the two probes co-fire during text generation. We asked the model to generate responses to 10 policy prompts ("As a U.S. politician, what is your position on immigration policy?" etc.) and scored each generated token with both probes simultaneously. If the probes are tracking the same latent variable, their token-level scores should be correlated across the sequence.

Across ten policy prompts the mean prompt-wise Pearson correlation is approximately 0.28, and the global token-level correlation (pooling all tokens across all prompts) is similar. For a probe derived from politician portraits—completely different inputs—to track the continuous variation of a text-based probe during language generation is a non-trivial finding.

Prompt 5 (federal taxation) and Prompt 7 (voting rights), which yielded the highest correlations (~0.4–0.45), also happen to produce the most politically differentiated text outputs—suggesting the ideological "temperature" of the response is what both probes are sensing.

Discussion

Interpretations and Limitations

What could explain the visual–textual alignment?

Multimodal training data is the most natural explanation. Models like Qwen3-VL are trained on image–text pairs scraped from the web, and the web has strong correlations between visual aesthetics and cultural politics. Rural landscapes, outdoor sports, hunting, and farming appear disproportionately in politically conservative media contexts; bright, urban, and diverse imagery appears more in progressive media. The model apparently absorbed these correlations.

Alternatively, the alignment may reflect a more fundamental property of high-capacity transformers: they tend to build universal semantic representations that bind across modalities. If the ideology dimension is genuinely a semantic axis (capturing something about worldviews, aesthetics, and values), the model's multi-modal pre-training may directly reuse such dimension from textual pretraining and apply it across modality.

Limitations

The Unsplash experiment involves no ground-truth labels: we cannot say whether a photograph of jellyfish is "actually" liberal. The DW score assigned is entirely a property of the model's internal representation, not an objective cultural fact. Replication with other VLMs and other training corpora would strengthen confidence in the generality of the findings.

The portrait probe is trained and evaluated on members of a single Congress (116th, 2019–21), a period of unusually sharp partisan polarization. Performance on earlier, less polarized sessions should be tested.

TL;DR — What We Found

  • A DW-NOMINATE probe trained on text activations transfers to visual image tokens from a multimodal VLM without any retraining.
  • Applied to 25,000 stock photos, the probe reveals systematic visual correlates of the liberal–conservative axis: arid + rugged + rural = conservative; colorful + aquatic + urban-diverse = liberal.
  • A probe trained solely on congressional portrait images achieves meaningful prediction of voting ideology—and Democrats vs. Republicans are separable from their official photos alone.
  • The textual and portrait probe directions are substantially aligned in activation space (mean cosine sim ≈ +0.4), and they co-fire token-by-token during generation (mean Pearson ≈ 0.28).
  • Taken together, the results suggest that a multimodal LLM encodes a unimodal ideological axis shared across language and vision—most likely absorbed from the correlated statistics of political media during pre-training.

What Comes Next

We are currently exploring several promising extensions to this work. For portrait generalization, following advice from my advisor Aaron, our next step is to evaluate using ranking metrics (like Pearson correlation) rather than absolute ideology values. This ranking-based approach may provide a more robust measure of how effectively the probe orders politicians across varying image domains.

We are also thinking about dissecting the Unsplash imagery further by increasing the number of clusters (k). This would allow us to isolate more granular image objects and see exactly what the model is classifying as liberal or conservative. However, given that our Unsplash sample leans so heavily toward nature photography, we may instead apply this method to ImageNet, where a vast array of common objects are immediately available with clear labels.

We also seem to be able to find a clear divide in the t-SNE plots of the unsplash embeddings that correspond to the liberal and conservative clusters. Is ideology association a major factor in how images are represented in the CLIP model used to generate those embeddings? Can we use a PCA to verify if the first or second principal component is aligned with the ideology dimension?

Also, it's interesting that we observed the probe correctly coloring people's figure or faces in the images with red or blue. Does this mean that the ViT encoder in the VLM not only mixes information across image patches, but also preserve token level information at each position? Is there a way to prove or quantify this?

Another fascinating extension of this work is audio probing. Vision and text are not the only ways models interact with the world—can we probe a "silence dimension" using audio data? What will steering on the silence dimension do to textual generation? Extending our linear probing framework into the auditory domain could reveal entirely new modal alignments.

More broadly, these findings have implications for how we think about value alignment in multimodal AI. If political ideology is structurally represented across modalities, then attempts to de-bias a model's textual responses may introduce untraced visual biases—and vice versa. Auditing AI systems for ideological representation may need to happen at the level of the shared latent geometry, not modality by modality.
